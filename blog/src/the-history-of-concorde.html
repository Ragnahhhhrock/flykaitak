Concorde was a joint British and French project that carried passengers across the Atlantic at twice the speed of sound for 27 years. Here is the story from the 1962 treaty to the last flight in 2003.

1962: a treaty to share the cost

On 29 November 1962 Britain and France signed an agreement to build a supersonic airliner together. Sharing the bill was the point: it was too big a risk for either country alone. The aircraft was shown to the public at Toulouse on 11 December 1967.

1969: first flights

The French prototype, 001, made its first flight on 2 March 1969. The British prototype, 002, followed from Filton on 9 April 1969. Concorde first went supersonic on 1 October 1969, and the test programme reached Mach 2 in 1970.

1976: into service

Certification came in 1975, and on 21 January 1976 the two national airlines began flying Concorde. One flew London to Bahrain, the other Paris to Dakar and on to Rio de Janeiro. Washington Dulles followed in May 1976, and New York's JFK on 22 November 1977.

Only 20 Concordes were ever built. Six were prototypes and development aircraft, and 14 flew for the airlines, seven each.

What made it Concorde

  • The ogival delta wing. A slender, curved delta wing works at Mach 2 and also lets the aircraft fly slowly enough to land. It lands nose-high and fast.
  • The droop nose. At that angle the pilots cannot see the runway through a fixed nose, so the nose tilts down for take-off and landing and lifts again for flight.
  • Engine intakes under the wing. Each wing carries one nacelle with two engines, tucked under the leading edge, with variable ramps that slow the air for the engines.
  • Reheat. Afterburners helped it take off and push through the sound barrier.
  • Cruise near Mach 2. It flew at roughly 50,000 to 60,000 feet, so a New York crossing took around three and a half hours.

2000: the Paris accident

On 25 July 2000 a Concorde crashed shortly after taking off from Paris Charles de Gaulle, near Gonesse. All 109 people on board and four people on the ground died. Concorde was grounded, modified and returned to passenger service in November 2001.

2003: the last flights

Passenger numbers and costs had changed after the accident. The French airline ended Concorde flights on 31 May 2003, and the British airline flew its last scheduled service on 24 October 2003. The final flight of all was G-BOAF from Heathrow to Filton on 26 November 2003.
