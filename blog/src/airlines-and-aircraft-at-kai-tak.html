In the 1990s Kai Tak handled a long list of carriers, from the Hong Kong home airlines to flag carriers from Europe, Australasia and Asia. This is a short guide to the airlines you would have seen on the apron, the aircraft they flew and what their jets looked like. Airline names, fleets and liveries are described in words, not drawn, so the sim stays free of brand artwork.

The aircraft you would see

Kai Tak's 47° turn and short final favoured wide-bodies with good low-speed handling. The mix in the 1990s ran from the 747-400 through the 777-200, the A330 and A340, the MD-11, the 767, the A300-600 and the 747-200/300, down to narrow-bodies such as the A320 and the 737 on regional routes. Fly Kai Tak flies the 747-400, 777-200, A330-300 and A340-300 today, with more types on the roadmap.

Cathay Pacific

History. Founded in 1946 by Roy Farrell and Sydney de Kantzow with a single war-surplus DC-3, Cathay Pacific grew with Hong Kong and became the colony's flag carrier. Swire took a major stake in 1948. By 1998 it was the airport's largest operator, and its flights were among the last to leave Kai Tak.

Aircraft. 747-400, 777-200, A330-300 and A340-300.

Livery. A white fuselage with a green cheatline along the windows and a tail carrying the green and white "brushwing", a stylised wing stroke in two tones of green.

Dragonair

History. Hong Kong Dragon Airways was founded in 1985 by shipping magnate Sir Y. K. Pao and partners to give Hong Kong a second home carrier, and it built a network across Asia and into mainland China.

Aircraft. A320 and A330-300, plus older narrow-bodies on short routes.

Livery. A white fuselage with a red tail carrying a dragon emblem.

Qantas

History. Qantas began in 1920 as Queensland and Northern Territory Aerial Services, flying outback mail and passengers before becoming Australia's international carrier. Its name is the old initials, turned into a word.

Aircraft. 747-400 and 767-300 on the Australia routes.

Livery. A white fuselage with a red tail and a white kangaroo, with the company name along the forward fuselage.

British Airways

History. British Airways formed in 1974 when BOAC and BEA were merged, and was privatised in 1987. It had flown to Hong Kong since the BOAC days, and the London run was one of the longest into Kai Tak.

Aircraft. 747-400.

Livery. In 1997 the airline swapped its single tail design for a collection of "world images", so a row of its jets at the terminal showed a different piece of art on each fin above a white fuselage with a dark blue belly.

Singapore Airlines

History. Singapore Airlines began in 1972 when Malaysia-Singapore Airlines split in two. It built a name for service and for new aircraft, and flew to Hong Kong almost from the start.

Aircraft. 747-400 and 777-200, with smaller twins on some rotations.

Livery. A pale, silver-white fuselage with a dark blue cheatline, a dark blue tail and a stylised yellow bird.

Thai Airways International

History. Thai Airways was founded in 1960 and became the Thai national carrier, gaining full government ownership in 1977 and merging with its domestic arm in 1988. Bangkok to Hong Kong was a busy trunk route.

Aircraft. A300-600, 747-400 and MD-11.

Livery. A white fuselage with a sweep of purple, magenta and gold stripes that rise onto the tail, which gave the jets a very bright look on the apron.

Japan Airlines

History. Japan Airlines was founded in 1951 and became the flag carrier in 1953. Its Tokyo and Osaka services to Hong Kong were among the busiest in Asia.

Aircraft. 747-400 and DC-10.

Livery. A white and grey fuselage with a red crane inside a circle on the tail, a design that has been its signature for decades.

And many more

The apron also saw carriers such as Air New Zealand, Korean Air, China Airlines, EVA Air, KLM, Lufthansa, United and Northwest, as well as cargo operators. After Kai Tak closed on 6 July 1998, every flight moved to the new airport at Chek Lap Kok.

A note on the sim. Fly Kai Tak keeps its aircraft in its own colours and does not show airline logos or real liveries. The descriptions above are for reading only.
